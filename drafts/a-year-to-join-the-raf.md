Picture applying for a job in September and being told you'll start the following September. Most people would take another offer in the meantime. For someone joining the Royal Air Force as a regular recruit, that is close to the typical experience.

In a [written answer on 8 September](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2026-08-28/24688), the Minister for Veterans and People gave the median time from submitting an application to starting basic training, for July 2025 to June 2026. It was 252 days for the Royal Navy, 247 for the Army and 356 for the RAF. The [UK Defence Journal reported it](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/raf-recruits-wait-356-days-to-start-training/) the next day.

The question had a second part. It also asked how long recruits wait between their medical assessment and the start of training. The MOD couldn't say, because that data is "not held centrally" in a form that can be compared across the three services. I found that half of the answer more interesting than the headline number.

### Where the year goes

The answer blames medical and security checks, the availability of specialist training places, and candidate choice. An [earlier answer from February 2025](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2025-02-10/30179) was more specific: waiting for GP records, medical deferment for candidates who need time to get fit, lining up basic training with the trade training that follows it, and the candidate's own job and housing situation. It also noted that Navy and RAF recruits need Security Check clearance before they join and Army recruits don't, which is one reason the MOD warns against comparing the services directly.

Each of those is a separate queue with a separate owner. GP records come from the NHS. Vetting runs to its own timetable, and training places depend on course dates planned long in advance.

Eliyahu Goldratt's *The Goal* (1984) explains why that matters. The output of any chain of dependent steps is set by its slowest step, which he called the constraint. Speeding up any other step only moves the queue. His first instruction is to find the constraint, because effort spent anywhere else is wasted.

Some of the reform effort has gone into the front of the process. The MOD's [February 2025 announcement](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/armed-forces-to-cut-red-tape-and-deliver-quicker-and-easier-recruitment-service) set an ambition of a conditional offer within 10 days and a training start date within 30. Those are real improvements for the candidate. But if the constraint for a given RAF trade is a clearance or a course place, a faster offer means the candidate knows sooner that they'll be waiting, and the overall time stays the same.

### Measuring the parts

James Womack and Daniel Jones, in *Lean Thinking* (1996), asked organisations to follow the value stream: every step a product passes through from order to delivery. Value stream mapping, the tool Mike Rother and John Shook set out in *Learning to See* (1999), records for each step how long the work takes and how long the item sits waiting before it. The waits tend to sit in the gaps between steps, where no single team is responsible for them.

To draw that map for recruitment you need the date each candidate reached each stage. The February 2025 answer shows why that is hard. Its figures were built by matching applications from two separate recruiting systems to intake records in a third, the Joint Personnel Administration system. Cases that couldn't be matched were left out, and the answer warned that each service's own recruiting teams calculate their numbers differently. The centre can see when a candidate applied and when they arrived. The months between are held by each service, in its own system and its own way.

The MOD knows this. Another [answer on 8 September](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2026-08-28/23359) said the Armed Forces Recruiting Programme is building a common data model to give "a single view of candidate progress" across all three services, so that delays and drop-outs can be spotted earlier. Dashboards and performance measures are [due from 1 April 2027](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2026-10-09/33005), after the new tri-service recruitment service takes over between [January and April 2027](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2026-10-09/30966). For now, the end-to-end view is a plan.

### What the wait costs

A median time to join only counts the people who joined. Anyone who gave up during those 356 days is missing from the figure.

The best published evidence on who gives up is old. The [National Audit Office found in 2018](https://www.nao.org.uk/press-releases/investigation-into-the-british-armys-recruiting-partnering-project/) that in 2017-18, 47% of Army applicants voluntarily dropped out of the process and 11% went on to join. The Army and its recruiting partner both believed the length of the process was a significant factor. In its 2025 announcement the MOD said fewer than one in ten applicants joined in 2023. Neither figure is broken down by stage, so neither tells you where in the year people leave.

### Why did the Navy and Army get faster?

The trend since 2022 is clear for two services. The Navy's median fell from 376 days to 252, and the Army's from 321 to 247, though the Army has barely moved since 2023. The RAF fell from 463 days in 2022 to 301 in 2024, then rose to 356. (The earlier figures cover years ending in September and the latest one a year ending in June, so the comparison is close but not exact.)

The minister credits the reforms for the Navy and Army improvement: faster offers, more than 100 outdated medical policies removed, digital access to medical records and better communication with candidates. Those reforms applied to all three services, and the RAF went the other way. The answer doesn't explain why, and I haven't found anything published that does.

One thing the figures can't separate is worth knowing. RAF intake rose [37% in the year to June 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/quarterly-service-personnel-statistics-2026/quarterly-service-personnel-statistics-1-july-2026). A measure taken on the day people start training will also rise when a service works through a backlog of older applications. Whether that happened here, the published data doesn't say.

### The case that it's working

There is a fair argument that none of this is a crisis. Medical and security standards exist for good reasons, especially for people who will work on aircraft or with classified material. Many applicants also choose to defer, to finish a course or work their notice, and that isn't a system failure.

The bigger point is that the overall numbers are improving. In the year to June 2026, 14,500 people joined the regular forces and 13,050 left. On [17 November](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-questions/detail/2026-11-04/40916) the minister said intake now exceeds outflow for the first time in several years. The RAF's regular strength grew by 840, more than the Navy (440) and the Army (170) combined. The service with the longest wait grew the most.

I take that seriously. But intake measures what the system produces, and the wait measures what each candidate goes through. The first can improve while the second gets worse, and the cost of the gap shows up in people who don't appear in either number.

### A test

The question I'd ask of any recruitment reform is a simple one. Can anyone tell you how long the whole journey takes, stage by stage, for everyone who started it, including the people who left? On the MOD's own account, nobody can yet. The test for the new recruitment service is whether it publishes the stage times and the drop-out at each stage alongside the headline median. Until then, it's hard to know whether a reform has sped up the constraint or one of the steps around it.
