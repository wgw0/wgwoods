Picture a household that decides to double its renovation budget. A new kitchen and a loft conversion. In the same month it lets go of the person who manages the builders: the one who writes the brief, compares the quotes, chases the plasterer and notices when the joists are the wrong size. The money is there. There's just nobody left to turn it into a house.

I kept thinking of that while reading the National Audit Office's report on the [Affordability of the Defence Investment Plan](https://www.nao.org.uk/reports/affordability-of-the-defence-investment-plan/), published on 25 November. I've already used the same report to write about [why honest people sign off optimistic numbers](/blog/why-honest-people-sign-off-optimistic-numbers/). This post is about a different part of it: whether the MoD has the people to spend the money at all.

### What the NAO actually found

The Defence Investment Plan sets out about £298 billion of spending over the four years to 2029-30. Much of the equipment side runs through the National Armaments Director Group (NADG), which the NAO describes as bringing together "several previously separate organisations which deliver and support defence equipment and infrastructure". Defence Equipment and Support is the best known of them.

During the planning process NADG cut £12.5 billion from its own four-year cost forecasts, so that they matched what it thought it and industry could actually deliver. Then, shortly before the plan was finished, it [warned](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Affordability-of-the-defence-investment-plan.pdf) that it was "already operating unsustainably due to the imbalance between programme scope and delivery capacity". It had an 18% shortfall against the capacity it needed and judged it had to get that down to 10%. In the NAO's words, "planned reductions to its workforce would make this problem significantly worse." NADG's own options were to spend more on contractors in the short term or to stop work on several important programmes, on top of those the plan had already paused or cancelled.

So the organisation responsible for buying the kit has said, in writing, that it doesn't have enough people to do what is already on its books.

### What the buying actually involves

The work looks a lot like the renovation manager's job, at a much larger scale. Someone has to turn a military need into a requirement a supplier can price. Commercial staff have to design and negotiate the contract, then manage it for years. Project managers hold the schedule and the risks together. Engineers have to check that what the supplier delivers is safe and does what was asked, which in defence can mean building the safety case for a ship or an aircraft before it enters service.

None of that can be switched on quickly. A good commercial lead on a complex programme has usually spent years learning how a particular supplier and its regulators behave. Cohen and Levinthal called the organisational version of this [absorptive capacity](https://doi.org/10.2307/2393553): an organisation can only use what it already has the knowledge to understand. A procurement team without the right people can't absorb a larger programme just because the budget line has grown.

The NAO makes the same point about timing. The plan pushes a large share of spending past 2030 (it assumes about 20% of project costs won't be spent as planned), which creates what the report calls a "bow wave" in the next Parliament. NADG "is aware that it will need to make sure its workforce is suitably sized and skilled before this spending increase". If it isn't, "projects will start more slowly, and military capabilities would be likely to enter service later than planned." The people have to arrive before the money does, and on the current plan the workforce is shrinking while the money is still to come.

### When a target meets a behaviour

The part of the report I found most interesting is a single paragraph about behaviour. The plan includes a 10% reduction in civil service workforce costs. In September 2025 a defence minister [described the ambition](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/mod-plans-10-cut-to-civil-service-costs-by-2030/) as "a productivity-driven ambition, not a headcount target". The NAO then found "emerging evidence that the civil service reduction targets were leading MSHQ [Military Strategic Headquarters] to replace some civil servants with military personnel, even though civil servants are cheaper to employ."

The logic is easy to follow. The target counts civil service costs. A uniformed officer doing the same desk job doesn't appear in that count, so the measured number goes down while the total cost to defence goes up. The NAO notes that this also conflicts with the MoD's own aim of moving military personnel out of administrative roles, such as HR, and into front-line ones.

This is Goodhart's law, usually paraphrased as "[when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1062798700002660)". The social scientist Donald Campbell put a sharper version in [1979](https://doi.org/10.1016/0149-7189(79)90048-X): the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it gets corrupted and the more it distorts the activity it was meant to monitor. Nobody in this story needs to be acting in bad faith. A manager told to reduce civil service costs, who still needs the work done, will find a way to get it done that the target doesn't see. The NAO says as much in its own words: "there is a risk that the efficiency measures could incentivise behaviours that help achieve targets without delivering underlying efficiencies."

### The case for targets

It would be too easy to stop there, so it's worth giving the other side a fair hearing. Contractors give real flexibility. An organisation facing a temporary peak in work can bring in specialists for a few years instead of hiring permanent staff it may not need after 2035. That is a sensible way to handle a bow wave.

Efficiency pressure also has value in its own right. Large organisations rarely get smaller or simpler without someone setting a number, and a vague instruction to "be more productive" is easy to agree with and easy to ignore. A target forces a conversation about which work matters.

Both points hold, but the NAO's evidence limits them. NADG reported that its ability "to recruit or buy in external assistance" has continued to tighten, and that delays to productivity investments, such as digital tooling, mean less of the reduced staffing is being offset. So contractors are a partial answer at best, and the productivity gains that would justify a smaller workforce haven't fully arrived yet.

### A test for any efficiency target

What I take from this is a simple question to ask of any efficiency target, in defence or anywhere else: what will it make people do?

A target describes an outcome. The people under it decide the behaviour, and they will usually choose the cheapest route to the number. If the cheapest route is to swap a civil servant for a more expensive officer, or to let a procurement team shrink just as its workload is about to grow, the target will be met and the purpose behind it missed. The household in my opening could hit its savings goal by letting the project manager go. It would still end up paying more for a kitchen that arrives late.
