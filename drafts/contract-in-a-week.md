---
title: 'A contract in a week. Then what?'
description: 'Defence has made it much faster to sign a contract with a small firm. The slow part was always what the buyer does next.'
pubDate: '2026-11-30'
---

Within a few days of each other this month, two NATO officials described how fast the alliance can now buy from small companies. Ryan Benitez, chief commercial officer of NATO's DIANA accelerator, told the Atlantic Future Forum in Oslo that DIANA can put a startup on contract ["not in a year, not in a month, but in a week if we have to"](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/natos-diana-says-it-can-put-startups-on-contract-in-a-week/). Nikos Loutas, NATO's director of innovation, then described the alliance's new counter-drone marketplace to journalists in İzmir: pre-competed products, fixed prices, and ["you can get a contract done in 24 hours"](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/nato-counter-drone-marketplace-offers-24-hour-contracts/).

A year, a month, a week, a day. That's a real change, and the people who built these routes deserve credit for it. But it made me ask what exactly has been made faster. A contract is the moment a buyer and a seller agree terms. Whether a soldier ever uses the thing depends on a lot that happens either side of that moment, and most of it sits inside the buying organisation.

### The front door got quicker

I wrote in August about [the valley of death](/blog/the-valley-of-death-revisited/), and how schemes like DASA and DIU found it far easier to fund prototypes than to get them bought. What's new since then is how much effort has gone into the very first step.

The counter-drone marketplace is a good example. NATO's Communications and Information Agency [launched it on 5 November](https://nato.int/en/news-and-events/articles/news/2026/11/05/nato-accelerates-procurement-for-counter-drone-technologies) with 16 systems from five companies, already competed, so allies can buy from the catalogue "without any further tender process". In the UK, the MoD on 27 November opened a [Test and Evaluation Marketplace](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/britain-opens-defence-test-ranges-to-small-firms-online/), an online portal where small firms can find and book ranges and facilities such as Porton Down. Access to MoD ranges has been a long-running complaint from small firms, so this fixes a real problem. Both make it easier for a company to get in. Neither says much about who, on the government side, wants what comes out.

### NATO now says the gap is further back

What struck me about Loutas's remarks is that he said this himself. NATO built DIANA to bring dual-use firms into defence, then the NATO Innovation Fund to back them, then a rapid adoption action plan and test ranges. "The missing piece," he said, "was scale."

The answer agreed at the [Ankara summit in July](https://nato.int/en/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/2026/07/08/nato-innovation-scale-up-package) is an innovation scale-up package with three parts: a published demand signal setting out the problems NATO wants solved, a push for banks and private capital to lend to defence firms, and a framework called the NATO Engine to match companies with spare factory capacity. Asked what Europe lacked, Loutas said the technology mostly exists and the question is whether allies are quick enough at "understanding how to effectively integrate all that as part of our defence capabilities".

Benitez made the same point more bluntly. DIANA works on four conditions for adoption: demand from warfighters, funding, legal frameworks and integration with the primes. DIANA can set those conditions up, Benitez said, "but it's up to you, the allies, the nations here, to actually catch them and pull them through."

In other words, NATO's own innovation people now place the constraint inside the national military organisations that have to catch what comes through the door.

### What catching involves

Everett Rogers' *Diffusion of Innovations* is mostly read for its adoption curve, but it also has a model of how organisations adopt new things. Adoption starts with agenda-setting, when someone inside the organisation notices a performance gap and goes looking for a fix. The decision to adopt comes in the middle. After it come the stages that take longest: restructuring the organisation and the innovation to fit each other, clarifying what the thing is for as people start using it, and finally routinising it, so it stops being a new thing and becomes part of normal work.

Put defence procurement against that model and the contract sits in the middle. Front-door reforms speed up the middle. Rogers' first stage is, in effect, pull: a unit that knows it has a problem and wants this particular fix. His last three stages need people with time to integrate the kit and train others on it, plus a budget line that pays for the second order and the spares.

The UK evidence on that back half isn't encouraging. The National Audit Office's [report on the Defence Investment Plan](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/Affordability-of-the-defence-investment-plan.pdf), published on 25 November, records the MoD's own procurement organisation saying the department "is not currently ensuring new technologies are developed into usable capabilities quickly enough". The same report says that organisation is already running an 18% shortfall in the capacity it needs, with planned workforce cuts set to make it worse. Some of the people who would have to catch new technology are already stretched.

### Someone has to pull

Ukraine's Brave1 offers a contrast, and the interesting part is organisational. At NATO Edge in İzmir, Cristian Coman of the NCIA called it ["fresh air"](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/brave1-was-fresh-air-for-nato-innovation-says-official/) for NATO's own efforts. Brave1 now works with more than 2,500 companies. But Coman also pointed at the other side of the arrangement: Ukraine has battalions and divisions that deal only with drones, and "this does not exist yet into the NATO structure".

That's the pull. A unit whose whole job is drones has a standing reason to look for better ones and people who can tell a good one from a bad one. Brave1 is a fast front door attached to a buyer that already wants what comes through it. Copying the front door without the buyer gets you a quick contract and an uncertain second order.

This is also where Cohen and Levinthal's idea of absorptive capacity applies to the buyer as much as to the supplier. An organisation's ability to use outside knowledge depends on what it already knows. A military customer that has never operated a class of technology will struggle to write a good requirement for it or to judge the bids that come back.

### When the process itself trips

The MoD's Project PANOPTES shows what that looks like. Run by UK Defence Innovation through the Commercial X Dynamic Market and launched in September, it sought an autonomous, vehicle-mounted counter-drone system, with lasers the preferred effector and up to £5 million for six-month contracts. On 24 November the MoD [abandoned it](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/project-panoptes-protecting-the-force-enabling-manoeuvre), two days before Stage 2 bids were due, after finding "issues with the Stage 1 Pass/Fail evaluation criteria and the evaluation methodology". It says it will return with a revised procurement.

The notice doesn't say what went wrong and I won't guess. What it does show is where the failure sat. The firms had bid. The fast commercial route was in place. The weak point was the buyer's own method for judging which bids were feasible and viable. At launch the ministry also put the indicative wider value at £125 million while stating that this was not an approved budget. That's a front door with no committed money behind it for scale.

### The case for speed

None of this makes front-door speed unimportant. For a small company with a few months of cash, the difference between a contract in a week and a contract in a year can decide whether it survives to see the second order at all. Loutas said venture capital in defence "was barely a thing" a few years ago, and a buyer that pays quickly is part of what makes a defence startup fundable. A fast first contract also gives a unit something to try, and sometimes trying is how a unit finds out it wants the thing.

So I'd keep the fast routes. My worry is that contracts are easy to count, so they become the measure of success, and the organisational work that decides adoption goes unmeasured.

### A test worth applying

When the next fast-contracting mechanism is announced, I'd ask one question about it: how many second orders has it produced? A first contract shows the front door works. A second order, paid for by the unit that used the first batch, shows that someone on the inside caught the technology and decided to keep it. That number is harder to publish, and it's the one that tells you whether anything has been adopted.
