What did you learn in your first ten years of work that you still assume is true? How long a decision should take, perhaps, or who you have to persuade before anything moves. Very little of it was taught on purpose. Most of it soaked in from the conditions you happened to be working in, and it tends to stay put long after those conditions have gone.

I was thinking about that while reading an [interview with Air Chief Marshal Sir Johnny Stringer](https://ukdefencejournal.org.uk/stringer-wants-nato-to-train-leaders-for-the-digital-age/), NATO's Deputy Supreme Allied Commander Europe, given to the UK Defence Journal at the NATO Edge 26 conference in İzmir. His argument was about exactly this, applied to the people at the top of the alliance.

### What a career teaches you

Stringer's point was that a typical 50-year-old general, admiral, air marshal or senior civil servant in NATO has spent 30 years shaped first by the post-Cold War peace dividend and then by two decades of counter-insurgency in places like Iraq and Afghanistan. The fighting was bloody, he said, and a lot of people were lost. "But we owned space. We owned cyber. We owned air. That's not the case now."

Much of what they learned on the job, he added, was learned outside an era of collective defence. Their exposure to digital technology also varies a lot depending on which path they took through their service. Given how fast things have moved in the last five years, he doesn't think NATO can keep relying on people to pick it up themselves: "If you're relying on people to be self-starters, autodidacts, they'll get it themselves by osmosis, I don't think that's fit anymore." His answer is targeted digital education, delivered by NATO itself because many smaller allies lack the education structures of the larger ones.

Management research has looked at this for a long time. Donald Hambrick and Phyllis Mason's 1984 paper on [upper echelons theory](https://doi.org/10.2307/258434) argued that organisations end up reflecting their top managers. Executives face more information than they can process, so they filter it through their own experience and values. What they've lived through shapes what they notice and how they interpret it. They used career background as a rough proxy for that filter: a leader who came up in finance sees different problems from one who came up in operations.

Apply that to Stringer's 50-year-olds. If your formative years were spent in campaigns where the air was uncontested and the networks were yours, then contested space and jammed communications aren't part of your instinctive picture of war. You can know about them perfectly well without them being the first thing you think of.

### Ambition and technology

On stage earlier in the day, Stringer had used the history of air power to argue that early military aviation was a story of ambition and imagination running ahead of the technology, until the technology overtook it. In the digital space, he said, commanders need to make sure their ambition stays in front. Asked whether it was in front yet, he said that in some areas the technology is currently ahead, though this was "not necessarily doom and gloom".

Put that next to his point about careers. At a conference where hundreds of companies were showing what is already possible, the constraint Stringer described sat with the buyers, in what senior leaders know to ask for. Industry can offer a capability, but someone has to recognise it as an answer to a problem they actually have, and then put their name to a requirement for it. In Stringer's account, that person's sense of what's possible was often formed in a different war.

Christopher Marquis and András Tilcsik's 2013 review of [imprinting](https://ecommons.cornell.edu/entities/publication/117d9719-40e9-42e2-a1bf-6a87a7de2e53) describes how people and organisations take on features of their environment during short "sensitive periods", such as the start of a career or a move into a new role, and how those features persist after the environment has changed. The idea goes back to Arthur Stinchcombe's 1965 observation that organisations carry the stamp of the era in which they were founded. Marquis and Tilcsik also note that imprints layer up, with each new sensitive period adding to the ones before it. That is the hopeful part: new imprints can be laid down later in a career, though they don't arrive on their own.

### Why osmosis stops working

Osmosis works when the thing you're absorbing is in the air around you. A lot of senior military learning has always worked that way, by serving alongside people and picking up the craft. For digital capability that has two problems.

The first is pace. If the useful knowledge changes meaningfully every couple of years, then what you absorbed as a major is out of date by the time you're a one-star. The second is unevenness, which Stringer named directly. Someone whose career ran through intelligence or cyber will have had a very different exposure from someone who spent it in command of conventional units. Osmosis depends on what you happen to be standing next to. In a senior team, that means the shared picture of what's possible is set by accident of career path.

### What deliberate learning looks like

The NHS has tried to tackle this head on. In 2020 NHS Providers, the membership body for NHS trusts, [signed a three-year contract with Health Education England](https://nhsproviders.org/news-blogs/news/nhs-providers-to-launch-new-digital-boards-development-programme) to run a Digital Boards programme with the consultancy Public Digital. It is aimed at trust boards, the executive and non-executive directors who sign off investment. Public Digital says the work has [reached over 3,000 board leaders across 154 events](https://public.digital/pd-insights/client-stories/nhs-boards). I can't tell from the outside how much it changed what those boards decided. But the design choice is the interesting part: someone decided that board-level understanding was a gap worth funding in its own right, and stopped waiting for boards to pick it up as digital spread through their hospitals.

### The case against retraining generals

There is a fair objection to all this. Senior leaders aren't meant to be technical. Their job is judgement: weighing risk and owning the consequences. A general doesn't need to understand how a data mesh works any more than they need to strip an engine. Spending scarce senior time on technical education may be a poor trade.

There is also a cheaper route. In 1999 Jack Welch had [hundreds of GE's senior leaders take on younger mentors](https://nextgov.com/digital-government/2011/11/taking-advice-from-the-young-and-wise/244782) to teach them about the internet, because he realised he and his management team didn't know enough about it. Reverse mentoring, or a trusted digital adviser in the room, brings current knowledge to the decision without asking a 50-year-old to retrain.

I think both points are right as far as they go. But judgement works on the options in front of you, and upper echelons theory suggests the options a leader can see are shaped by what they've lived through. An adviser helps most when the leader knows what question to ask them. Welch's scheme put the learning on the senior leaders themselves, which seems closer to what Stringer is asking for than either formal retraining or leaning on an adviser.

### A practical test

The test I take from this applies well beyond NATO. In any organisation trying to adopt new technology, ask two things. Who here decides what's possible? And when did they last learn something new about it, on purpose, from someone who knows more than they do?

If the honest answer to the second is "years ago, mostly on the job", the organisation is relying on osmosis. Stringer's view is that osmosis no longer keeps up, and I find that hard to argue with.
